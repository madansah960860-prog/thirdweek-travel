Five steps from a rough idea to a plan you can navigate by

About twenty minutes of work, and no account until you want to save. This page states what happens at each step and what the software is measuring underneath it.

Open the planner See what Atlas Pro adds

Reads the same on a phone, on a laptop, and on paper.

Step one: state your constraints, not your destination

Constraints first. Nearly every planning failure we see starts with a destination chosen early and a constraint discovered late.

The four readings we take first

  • Vacation days available. Counting the travel days at each end, or not, as you prefer. The default counts them.
  • Rough total budget in USD. A range is enough. The Ledger uses it to flag drift, never to block you.
  • Departure airport. A trip out of Newark and a trip out of Boise are different trips, even to the same destination.
  • Party size and mobility. Two adults, a family with a stroller and a group of six move at measurably different speeds.

Why your home airport moves the whole plan

A nonstop out of a large hub can make a five-day international trip work. Two connections out of a regional airport usually cannot, because most of two days goes to getting there and back.

Lodestar Atlas uses the airport to set the first and last Dayline honestly. Arrival day runs on low energy and departure day is mostly logistics, so both are built lighter without you asking.

Say so if you hold TSA PreCheck or Global Entry. Airport buffers drop by roughly 30 minutes on domestic departures and 45 minutes on an international return.

Step two: open the Atlas, or start from an empty board

Begin from a researched destination, from a finished sample plan, or from an empty three-day board. All three lead to the same place.

The Atlas is our researched stop library. Every destination in it carries the places we would put on a first visit, each with an opening time, a realistic visit duration, an entry cost in USD, and a note on when it fills up. You are under no obligation to use any of them.

Typing your own stop is a first-class action, not a fallback. Give it a name and a duration and it behaves exactly like a stop pulled from the Atlas.

Step three: build the Daylines and read True Pace

This is the step other planners leave out. A list of nine things you want to see is not a plan. A plan knows how long it takes to get from each one to the next.

A measured leg under every stop

Driving, walking or transit, with the distance in miles. Times assume ordinary traffic and ordinary parking, not an empty road at 5 AM.

A flag when the day will not fit

When stops plus travel pass about eleven active hours, True Pace marks the Dayline. You can override the flag. Most people move one stop instead.

A check against opening hours

Any stop scheduled before it opens or after it closes is highlighted. Seasonal closures on Atlas stops are printed on the stop itself.

The rule True Pace is built on

Two anchor stops per Dayline, one flexible stop, and one meal you have actually thought about. Past that, the day gets worse rather than fuller. The plans travelers finish are consistently the ones with the fewest items per day, which surprised us as well.

Under the hood

What True Pace assumes when it measures a day

Every estimate rests on assumptions. These are ours, written down so you can correct them when your trip is the exception.

On the road

  • Ordinary traffic, not best case. Drive times assume the road as it usually is at that hour, including the slow stretch through town.
  • Parking counts as driving. Ten minutes is added at any stop where you are likely to circle for a space or walk in from a lot.
  • Fuel and rest stops are real. Any leg over three hours picks up a 20-minute break, because you will take one whether the plan says so or not.

On foot and on transit

  • Walking at 3 mph, flat. Hills, snow and a stroller all slow that down, and none of them are in the number.
  • Transit includes the wait. Headways are taken from the published timetable, with a half-headway wait added at each transfer.
  • A meal is 60 to 90 minutes. A sit-down dinner in a country where the check arrives slowly is closer to two hours. Adjust it on the stop.

When to overrule the instrument

True Pace does not know that your flight lands at 6 AM, that a festival has closed the main street, or that your group moves faster than most. Every leg is editable. Change the number, and the rest of the Dayline recalculates around it.

Step four: the Ledger totals itself as you build

Every stop carries an estimated cost per person. The Ledger adds them up per Dayline and across the trip, multiplied by your party size, as you work.

These are estimates and we label them as estimates. Entry fees change, restaurants change, exchange rates move. What the figure is good for is comparison: it shows that swapping one guided tour for a self-guided walk saves more than skipping three lunches.

What the Ledger counts, and what it leaves out

  • Counted: activity and entry costs, tours you have added, meals entered as stops, and local transit legs.
  • Counted on Atlas Pro: a separate lodging line and a fuel estimate for every driving route.
  • Left out: international flights, travel insurance, souvenirs and tips.
  • Left out: anything you have not put on a Dayline. The Ledger does not invent spending you have not planned.

Step five: carry the Field Export where there is no signal

A plan that lives only in a browser tab is a plan you will lose in a gravel lot outside Vík with one bar of service.

  • Print from any browser. The print stylesheet drops the navigation, the ads and the footer, and keeps the Daylines.
  • Atlas Pro takes the Field Export, a PDF sized for a phone screen with each Dayline on its own page.
  • Atlas Pro shares one live link with everyone travelling. An edit shows up for the whole group at once.

Your first trip out of the country, in deadline order

Every task a US traveler has to clear before leaving the country, sorted by how soon it comes due. Work down the list.

By Teodoro Salas Published 02/04/2026 Updated 07/29/2026 13 min read

Roughly half of American adults have never held a passport. If your first trip outside the country is coming up, the worry is rarely the destination. It is the paperwork, the airport, the money and the phone. This guide takes all four in the order they come due, with the numbers and lead times attached.

Six months out: get the passport moving

Start here. Every other item on this page moves faster than this one.

A first adult passport has to be applied for in person at an acceptance facility, usually a post office or a county clerk's office. Bring proof of citizenship, a photo ID, a passport photo and the fee. Routine processing has run between six and eight weeks in recent years, with expedited service at roughly two to three weeks. Both figures move. Check the published times on travel.state.gov rather than trusting any third party, this page included.

The rule that catches people is validity, not expiration. Many countries require a passport valid for at least six months past your date of entry, and some airlines will deny boarding on that alone. A passport expiring in four months is functionally expired for most international travel. Go look at the date now, before you read the next paragraph.

Some destinations also want a visa or an electronic travel authorization even for a short tourist stay, and several regions have brought in new pre-travel authorization systems. Requirements change with little warning. Read the US Department of State country information page for your destination once when you book, and again a month before you fly.

Three months out: PreCheck or Global Entry

These two get confused constantly. For a trip abroad, the difference decides which one you should buy.

TSA PreCheck speeds up departure security inside the United States. Shoes and belt stay on, the laptop stays in the bag, and the line is usually far shorter. It does nothing for you on the way back into the country.

Global Entry speeds up re-entry into the United States through customs and immigration, at a kiosk instead of the main queue. It includes PreCheck. For travel abroad it is the better buy, because the arrival queue at a large US airport after a transatlantic flight is where the real delay sits.

Both need an application, a background check and an in-person interview. The interview is the bottleneck. Slots at busy airports can be months out, though enrollment on arrival at some airports lets you finish the interview when you land from a trip abroad. Apply early. If you already carry a credit card that reimburses the application fee, use that card.

Traveling as a family changes the math. Children under a certain age can generally use the PreCheck lane alongside an enrolled adult. Global Entry does not work that way: every person needs their own membership, infants included.

How early to be at the airport

For an international departure from a large US hub: three hours before your flight without PreCheck, about two and a quarter hours with it. Add 30 minutes if you are checking bags at a peak time. Add another 30 for a Friday or a holiday period. Arriving early costs you a coffee. Arriving late costs you the trip.

Two months out: cards and cash

Three decisions. None of them complicated.

Carry a card with no foreign transaction fee. The standard fee runs around three percent of every purchase. On a $3,000 trip that is $90 handed over for nothing. Most travel credit cards waive it. Apply early enough that the physical card lands before you do.

Always take the local currency. When a terminal or an ATM abroad offers to charge you in dollars, decline. That option is dynamic currency conversion. It applies a poor exchange rate and is a fee wearing the costume of a convenience. Choosing local currency hands the conversion to your card network at a much better rate.

Pull cash from a bank ATM on arrival, never from an exchange counter. Airport exchange bureaus carry the worst rates you will meet anywhere on the trip. Withdraw a modest amount from a bank-branded ATM once you land and put everything else on a card. Japan and parts of Germany still run more on cash than the US does. Iceland and Sweden are close to cashless.

Tell your bank you are traveling if it still asks for that. Carry a second card on a different network and store it separately from the first. A frozen card in a country where you know nobody is a long afternoon.

One month out: the phone

Do not switch on your carrier's international day pass by reflex. Price it against the alternatives first. The gap is wide.

Most US carriers sell an international plan by the day, roughly $10 to $12 per line per day. Two travelers on a two-week trip is real money. An eSIM data plan from a specialist provider usually costs a fraction of that for the same allowance, and a modern phone runs one alongside your existing line, so your US number keeps taking texts and calls.

Whichever you pick, do three things before you leave. Download offline maps for every area on the route. Pull a Field Export of the plan so it opens with no signal, or print it. Save each lodging address in the local language where that applies. A driver who does not read English can read the address on your screen.

Two weeks out: insurance and copies

Travel insurance is worth buying for a first trip abroad, and the reason is medical, not baggage. Most US domestic health plans give limited coverage or none outside the country, and Medicare generally gives none. A policy covering emergency medical treatment and medical evacuation typically runs four to eight percent of your trip cost. Read the exclusions, particularly around pre-existing conditions and adventure activities.

Copy your passport photo page, your insurance policy number and your credit cards. Keep one set in your luggage, away from the originals, and one in cloud storage you can reach from a borrowed phone. Leave a third copy with someone at home.

US citizens can also register the trip with the State Department's STEP program, which puts you on the local embassy's contact list if something goes wrong. Five minutes, no cost.

Twenty-four hours out: four checks

  1. Check in online and confirm the seat. Airlines reassign seats more often than you would think. Finding out at the gate is worse than finding out at home.
  2. Confirm the departure terminal, not just the airport. Large airports run separate international terminals, and some airlines operate out of more than one of them.
  3. Weigh the bag at home. International allowances are often lower than domestic ones, and overweight fees at the counter are set to hurt.
  4. Charge everything and put the adapter in the carry-on. A plug adapter inside a checked bag that does not arrive ruins night one.

Landing: the first two hours

Follow the signs for passport control with your passport already out. Answer the officer plainly: where you are staying, how long you are visiting. Collect the bag, clear customs, and only then start looking for transport.

Use the official taxi rank, a pre-booked transfer, or the airport train. Turn down anyone offering a ride in the arrivals hall. That holds at most large airports worldwide, and it is the most common way a first-time traveler gets overcharged.

Then do very little for the rest of the day. Jet lag after an overnight eastbound flight is real, and a first day spent slowly outdoors in daylight resets you faster than a first day spent chasing four museums. The Lodestar Atlas planner builds the arrival Dayline light for this reason, and True Pace flags it if you try to load it up. The destination pages in the Atlas mark which sights are worth arrival-day energy and which are not.

The part nobody mentions

A first trip abroad feels harder than it turns out to be. Airports elsewhere are laid out much like airports at home. Signage in most tourist destinations is bilingual. The people behind the counters handle confused visitors all day and are not surprised by you. The parts that genuinely need preparation are the four above, and all four are finished before you lock your front door.

After that it is only a trip.

Six mistakes we see most often

Every one of these is cheap to avoid a month out and expensive to fix at the airport.

Booking the flight before the passport

Routine processing has run six to eight weeks. A nonrefundable ticket does not move that queue by a single day.

Reading only the expiration date

Many countries want six months of validity past your entry date. A passport expiring in four months is already useless to them.

Letting the terminal charge you in dollars

Dynamic currency conversion sets its own rate, and it is never the good one. Choose the local currency every time you are asked.

Taking the carrier day pass by default

At $10 to $12 per line per day, two travelers on two weeks is real money. Price an eSIM against it before you leave.

Assuming health coverage travels with you

Most US domestic plans cover little or nothing abroad, and Medicare generally covers none of it. The medical cover is the reason to buy the policy.

Loading up the arrival Dayline

You land off an overnight flight with about half a day of usable energy. One stop, daylight, an early night. True Pace will tell you the same thing.

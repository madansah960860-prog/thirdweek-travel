The Utah loop is decided by the driving between the parks

The Utah and Arizona loop is the great American road trip, and the one most often crammed into too few days. What it costs, which months hold up, and how far apart these places actually sit once you measure them.

  • 7 to 14 days
  • Rental car required
  • About $165 per person per day
See the three Daylines Plot your own

No passport. No jet lag. Nothing to convert into dollars.

The shape of the loop

Utah's five national parks, usually called the Mighty 5, run in a rough arc across the southern half of the state: Zion, Bryce Canyon, Capitol Reef, Arches and Canyonlands. Add the Grand Canyon's North or South Rim in Arizona and the loop grows into something most people attempt in a week and should give ten days.

Scale is what US travelers underestimate here, including people who drive long distances at home. Zion to Arches is 340 miles and about five and a half hours with no stops. Fitting both into a four-day trip spends two of those days looking at asphalt. The advice we give most often on this route is to cut a park rather than compress the driving.

The parks have also changed how they handle crowds. Several now require timed entry, shuttle reservations or lottery permits through the peak months, and those windows open on fixed dates months ahead. Missing one is the most common way a Southwest trip comes apart, and it is entirely preventable.

The loop works across a wide range of fitness levels. Every park on it puts real scenery within a short paved walk of a parking lot, and every park also holds strenuous hikes for anyone who wants them.

Standing facts

Fly into
Las Vegas for the western parks. Salt Lake City or Grand Junction for the eastern ones.
Park pass
An America the Beautiful annual pass covers entry to all of them and pays for itself by the third park.
Vehicle
Any standard car handles the paved routes. High clearance is needed only on specific backcountry roads.
Cell service
Unreliable to nonexistent inside the parks. Download offline maps before you leave town.
Elevation
The Bryce Canyon rim sits above 8,000 feet. You will feel it on the first day.
Time zone
Utah runs on Pacific Time. Most of Arizona does not observe daylight saving.

Fit check

What this loop is good for, and what it is not

More Southwest trips fail on fit than on budget. The loop is superb for some travelers and a poor use of a week for others, and the difference is usually visible before anyone books a flight.

A good fit

  • Mixed fitness levels
  • Families with a car
  • Night skies
  • Photography
  • Ten days or more

Every park here puts a serious view within a short paved walk, so a group with different capabilities can share the same day. Arches and Canyonlands are certified International Dark Sky Parks, and October light is the best of the year for a camera. Give the loop ten days and it holds five parks comfortably.

A poor fit

  • Under five days
  • No rental car
  • July and August
  • Booking last minute
  • Needing a signal

There is no rail and almost no bus service between gateway towns, so a trip without a car does not work at all. Under five days you are paying for asphalt. Summer highs pass 100°F in Moab and the inner canyons. Timed entry and lottery permits open months ahead, and cell coverage inside the canyons is close to nonexistent.

Which months are worth it

The desert offers two comfortable seasons, one quiet cold one, and one that is genuinely dangerous.

April to May: the window we plot first

Highs in the 70s and 80s, wildflowers at the lower elevations, and full access to the trails. Bryce can still be holding snow in early April. Reservation systems are running, so book the hour a window opens.

Plot it if: you are here to hike, this is a first visit, or you are traveling with family.

September to October: the other good window

The summer heat breaks, crowds thin out after Labor Day, and October light is the best of the year for a camera. Higher-elevation trails can take early snow by late October.

Plot it if: you are shooting photographs, or steering around school holidays.

June to August: hot enough to be dangerous

Temperatures routinely pass 100°F in Moab and the inner canyons. Monsoon storms through July and August bring flash flooding. Hiking narrows to before 10 AM and after 5 PM.

If the dates are fixed: start every hike at sunrise and carry far more water than feels necessary.

November to March: cold and empty

Very few people, snow sitting on red rock, and some park roads closed. Bryce gets genuinely cold, with highs near freezing. Daylight is short, so plot fewer stops per day.

Plot it if: you have been before and you want the overlooks to yourself.

Lines nobody budgets for

  • One-way rental drop fees, which can pass $200 between Las Vegas and Salt Lake City.
  • A cooler, camp chairs and a stove, if you are camping and flying in.
  • Laundry mid-trip, because desert dust ends up in everything.
  • An extra tank of gas. Stations sit far apart and are priced accordingly inside park corridors.

When the permit windows open

Timed entry and permit systems shift from season to season. As a general shape:

  • Arches timed entry: released a few months out, with a small next-day release
  • Zion Angels Landing permit: a seasonal lottery, plus a day-before lottery
  • The Wave at Coyote Buttes: separate advance and daily lotteries, entered independently
  • In-park campgrounds: six months out on recreation.gov, and gone within minutes

Confirm the current dates and rules on the official National Park Service and recreation.gov pages before you book anything else.

Driving it

This is a driving trip and nothing else. There is no rail, and bus service between gateway towns is minimal. Pick the car up at the airport and expect to put 1,200 to 1,800 miles on it across ten days.

Shuttles and timed entry

Zion requires a shuttle to reach the main canyon for most of the year, and private vehicles are not permitted beyond a certain point. Bryce runs an optional shuttle. Arches uses timed entry instead of a shuttle. Check each park's current system against your dates.

Navigating with no signal

Cell coverage disappears inside the canyons and across long stretches between towns. Download offline maps for the whole region before you leave, carry the Field Export so the plan works without a signal, and keep a paper map in the glovebox. Do not count on a navigation app to find a trailhead.

Filling the tank

Fill up in every gateway town rather than waiting for the light. The stretch between Capitol Reef and Moab has long gaps, and prices inside the park corridors run well above the interstate average.

The two hours that matter

Midday in the desert means flat light and high heat. Build each Dayline around the two golden hours and give the middle of the day to driving, eating and resting. This is not a preference. It is how the landscape works.

Where to base each night

Springdale for Zion. You can walk to the park entrance and the shuttle.

Bryce Canyon City or Tropic for Bryce. Tropic costs less and sits 20 minutes out.

Torrey for Capitol Reef. Small and quiet, and the only real option.

Moab for Arches and Canyonlands. More lodging and food than anywhere else on the loop.

In-park lodges at Zion and Bryce open roughly 13 months out. Set a calendar reminder the day you pick your dates.

What actually goes wrong out here

Search and rescue call-outs in these parks come down to heat, water and navigation. Wildlife barely registers.

Water, by the gallon

One gallon per person per day is the summer minimum, and more than that if you are hiking. Carry it in the car as well as on the trail. Dehydration is the leading reason people need help out here.

Timing around the heat

Rangers close some trails to daytime hiking in summer, and they have good reason to. If a hike will not be finished by 10 AM, start earlier or move it to the evening.

Flash floods in slot canyons

From July through September, a storm miles away can fill a slot canyon in minutes. Check the daily flash flood potential rating at any visitor center, and treat it as binding.

Unrailed drop-offs

Most overlooks have no railings at all. Falls are the leading cause of death in Grand Canyon and a regular occurrence in Canyonlands. Keep children within arm's reach.

The living soil underfoot

The dark crusty ground between plants is cryptobiotic soil, and it takes decades to regrow once broken. Stay on the trails and on slickrock. Rangers will tell you the same thing.

If it goes wrong

Call 911 wherever you have service. Many trailheads carry emergency call boxes. A satellite messenger is genuinely worth the money for anyone hiking alone.

Leave your route and expected return time with someone who is not on the trip.

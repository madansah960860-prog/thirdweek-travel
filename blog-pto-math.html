Nine PTO days, placed well, is seventeen days off

Most Americans hold between 10 and 20 vacation days a year. Where you put them on the calendar matters more than how many you were given.

By Nadia Brantley Published 03/11/2026 Updated 08/14/2026 11 min read

The United States is the only large wealthy country with no statutory minimum paid vacation. Most full-time workers get somewhere between ten and twenty days, and a meaningful share of those days go unused every year. Against that, the difference between a good travel year and a frustrating one is usually a scheduling decision made in January, not a booking decision made in June.

This guide is about placement. It assumes a fixed, modest number of days, that you cannot simply ask for six weeks, and that your employer runs a normal approval process. Nothing here requires anyone to bend a policy for you.

Key numbers

PTO days spent
9 days
Calendar days off
17 days
US federal holidays
11
Transit, trip abroad
1.5 days

Start with the arithmetic

A five-day work week comes bracketed by two free days on either side. Any block of vacation days you take inherits those weekends for nothing. Nine consecutive weekdays off, taken as two full work weeks plus one day, reads as seventeen calendar days once you count the weekend before, the weekend in the middle and the weekend after.

In practice: request the Monday of week one through the Friday of week two, plus the following Monday. Nine PTO days spent. The break runs from the Saturday before through the Sunday nine days later, which is seventeen consecutive days away from work. That last Monday carries more weight than it looks. Drop it and you get sixteen days for eight, still a strong ratio, but you lose the recovery day at home before you walk back in.

The same rule scales down. Four PTO days taken Tuesday through Friday, with a federal holiday on the Monday, produces a nine-day break. Two PTO days on a Thursday and Friday produce a four-day weekend, which is long enough for a real domestic trip.

Build against the holiday calendar

The United States has eleven federal holidays. Most private employers observe six to eight of them. Every observed holiday that lands on a Monday or a Friday is a free day you can anchor a block to.

The holidays worth the most are the ones already sitting next to a weekend. Memorial Day and Labor Day are always Mondays. Independence Day, Veterans Day and Christmas move year to year, and in some years they land where a four-day request turns into nine days off.

Thanksgiving is the best stretch on the American calendar and also the worst week of the year to travel. Most employers give the Thursday and the Friday. Add the Monday, Tuesday and Wednesday and you have nine days off for three PTO days. The catch is that airfare, highway traffic and airport crowding all peak at once. Our advice: use Thanksgiving week for a driving trip within about 400 miles of home, and save the flying for a week nobody is fighting you for.

The quieter version is to take the week after a holiday instead of the week containing it. Demand drops off a cliff right after Labor Day, and again through the first two weeks of December. You get shoulder-season airfare without standing in line behind everyone who had the same idea.

One year, worked through

Fifteen PTO days, placed on purpose, can carry four separate trips totaling 34 days away from work: a nine-day trip in late April using four days, a four-day weekend in June using two days, a seventeen-day trip in late September using nine days, and a four-day weekend at Thanksgiving using zero days beyond the holiday itself. Same allowance. Roughly double the time away compared with three scattered single weeks.

The two windows worth booking into

Late April to mid May, and mid September to late October. Both sit just outside the summer peak, and both win on nearly every measure that matters.

Airfare drops once school is back in session. Lodging that shows no availability in July opens up. Timed-entry slots and lottery permits become obtainable instead of theoretical. Weather across most of Europe, Japan and the American West is at its most reliable. The places themselves are also better: a national park in late September and the same park on the Fourth of July are, in practice, two different destinations.

The trade-off is real. If you have school-age children, neither window is open to you. The next best move is the first week of summer break rather than the middle of it, because the crowding curve is not symmetric. The second week of June is measurably quieter than the second week of July at nearly every US destination.

Count the travel days honestly

This is where most plans break. A seventeen-day trip is not seventeen days on the ground.

An overnight flight from the East Coast to Europe lands around 8 AM local time. You will not be functional. Plot one light stop, an early night, and count the day as a half. On the return, a flight leaving Europe at noon reaches the US in the afternoon local time, and that day is gone outright. Transpacific routes cost you more in both directions.

Domestic math is kinder but not free. A morning flight, a rental car pickup and a drive to the first town eats most of a day. The rule we plan by: subtract 1.5 days from any international trip and 0.75 days from any domestic one before you decide how many places fit.

The Lodestar Atlas planner applies this for you. Arrival and departure Daylines are built light by default, and True Pace flags them if you load them up anyway. Override it when your flight times genuinely allow more, and leave it alone when they do not.

Getting the request approved

Long requests get denied for predictable reasons. Most of those reasons are avoidable.

  • Ask early. Requests filed three to six months out clear at much higher rates than requests filed six weeks out. Your absence gets planned around instead of absorbed.
  • Steer around the crunch. Every team has one: quarter close, the annual audit, a launch, open enrollment. Asking around it costs you nothing and removes the main objection before it is raised.
  • Bring the coverage plan with you. Naming who covers what turns the conversation from a decision into a signature.
  • Split the ask if you have to. If nine consecutive days is a hard no, two separate five-day blocks in the same quarter is usually a yes, and it still gives you two nine-day breaks.
  • Book flights after approval, never before. A nonrefundable ticket is a poor way to open the conversation, and it will be remembered longer than the trip.

If your policy says unlimited

Unlimited policies reliably produce less time off than fixed allocations. There is no number to use up and no accrual to lose, so nothing pushes back. If you have one, set your own number in January, write it down, and put the blocks on the calendar before anything else claims them.

Fifteen to eighteen days is a workable target, placed in the same holiday-adjacent pattern described above. Put each approved block in the shared calendar the day it is approved. A trip that exists on a calendar in February happens far more often than a trip that exists only as an intention.

The days you never spend

Rolling days into next year sounds prudent and usually is not. Accrual caps, policy changes and job changes all eat banked time, and the year you were saving for has a habit of not arriving. If carryover is allowed, carry enough to bridge one long trip and spend the rest.

The point underneath all of this is plain. The constraint most Americans face is not money and it is not appetite. It is a small number of days, spent badly. Placing them well costs nothing, takes about twenty minutes with a calendar, and returns more than any other travel decision you will make this year.

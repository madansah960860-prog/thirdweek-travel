What two weeks in the parks actually costs

Fourteen days, two people, five parks in the American Southwest. Priced line by line in three styles: camping, mid-range, and park lodges.

By Imogen Farrow Published 04/22/2026 Updated 08/02/2026 12 min read

A national parks road trip carries a reputation as the cheap American vacation. It does come in under a comparable trip to Europe, but the gap is narrower than most people expect. The reason is nearly always the same: the lines that are invisible at planning time and unavoidable on the road.

What follows is the full breakdown for one specific trip: fourteen days, two travelers, flying into Las Vegas and out of Salt Lake City, covering Zion, Bryce Canyon, Capitol Reef, Arches and Canyonlands. Roughly 1,450 miles of driving. Every figure is a planning estimate in USD, current as of the update date at the top of this page. Verify anything you are about to book.

The four lines that carry the budget

1. The vehicle

A mid-size car or small SUV picked up in Las Vegas and dropped in Salt Lake City runs roughly $70 to $110 per day in shoulder season, before taxes and fees. Across fourteen days that is $980 to $1,540.

Then the one-way drop fee, which on this route commonly adds $150 to $300. It is the most overlooked line in any Southwest budget. Flying in and out of the same airport removes it outright, and costs you a longer final driving day. Returning to Las Vegas instead adds about 4 hours 30 minutes behind the wheel and saves several hundred dollars. For two people that is usually the better trade.

Turn down the rental company's insurance only once you have confirmed your own cover. Many US credit cards carry secondary collision coverage on rentals, and many personal auto policies extend to rentals inside the country. Check both at home, not at the counter with a line behind you.

2. Gas

At 1,450 miles and 28 miles per gallon you burn about 52 gallons. At an average of $3.60 per gallon, that is roughly $187. An SUV at 22 miles per gallon takes it to about $237.

Two adjustments apply. Gas inside park corridors and small gateway towns sits well above the interstate average, sometimes by 60 cents or more per gallon, and you will buy some of it there because there is nothing else for 40 miles. Idling in an entrance queue on a spring morning also burns fuel without covering ground. Put 15 percent on top of the arithmetic.

3. Where you sleep

This line decides which of the three trips you are actually taking.

Camping: in-park campgrounds generally run $20 to $35 per night for a site. Thirteen nights lands at $260 to $455 for two people sharing. Availability is the constraint, not price. Sites at the popular parks are released six months out and sell out in minutes. Dispersed camping on nearby BLM land is free and legal in many areas, and asks a lot more self-sufficiency of you.

Mid-range hotels in gateway towns: $130 to $220 per night in shoulder season, more in summer. Thirteen nights runs $1,690 to $2,860. Springdale, Moab and Bryce Canyon City all price far above what their size suggests, because demand sits well past supply and everyone knows it.

In-park lodges: $200 to $320 per night, booking roughly thirteen months out. Thirteen nights is $2,600 to $4,160. What the money buys is position. On a trip built around sunrise, position is worth more than it reads on paper.

4. Eating

Two people taking one restaurant meal a day and running breakfast and lunch out of a cooler spend around $65 to $85 per day, or $910 to $1,190 across the trip. Cooking everything at camp drops that to roughly $35 to $45 per day. All three meals out in gateway towns, where the options are few and priced to match, pushes past $150 per day.

A cooler is the best $40 on the whole list. Buy groceries at a full-size supermarket in Las Vegas or St George before you enter the park corridor. The general stores in gateway towns stock a narrow selection at a serious markup, and they are not being unreasonable about it.

The Ledger so far

Camping: roughly $2,200 for two people across fourteen days. Mid-range: roughly $4,100. Park lodges: roughly $5,600. None of the three includes flights.

Six costs that never reach the Ledger

These are the lines that turn a $4,000 estimate into a $4,900 receipt.

  1. The one-way rental drop fee. $150 to $300 on this route. Listed above and repeated here, because it tends to appear at the counter rather than at booking.
  2. Timed entry and permit fees. Small on their own, from a few dollars for a reservation to a lottery fee for one specific hike, but they stack across five parks. Budget $60 to $120 over two weeks, and remember that a lottery fee is charged whether or not you win.
  3. State parks the federal pass does not cover. Dead Horse Point, Goblin Valley, Valley of Fire and Snow Canyon each charge on their own, generally $10 to $20 per vehicle. Budget $60.
  4. Laundry mid-trip. Fourteen days of desert dust puts a laundromat stop on the plan around day seven. Budget $15 to $25 and about 90 minutes out of that Dayline.
  5. Gear you did not know you needed. A second gallon jug, a wide-brim hat, a better headlamp, more sunscreen, a tire gauge. Budget $80 to $150 and plan on spending it.
  6. The bad-weather day. Roughly one day in fourteen gets rained out, snowed on or closed. What fills it is something indoors and paid for: a museum, a long lunch, a motel night in a town you had no plans to sleep in. Budget $150.

Together those six add $500 to $800 to a two-week Southwest trip. Fold them into the running total and the honest figures come out at roughly $2,800 camping, $4,800 mid-range and $6,300 for park lodges. Those are the numbers to plan against.

What the America the Beautiful pass covers

The America the Beautiful annual pass covers entry to every federal park site for one vehicle and everyone in it. Paying at the gate instead runs $30 to $35 per vehicle for seven days. On a five-park route you break even after three and are clearly ahead after five. Buy it, online in advance or at the first entrance gate.

Now the limits. The pass does not cover campgrounds, timed-entry reservation fees, lottery permits, in-park lodging, shuttle fees where they are charged, or state parks. It covers the entrance gate. Nothing past it.

Where the real savings are

Ranked by what each one returns on this specific route.

  • Fly in and out of the same airport. Kills the drop fee and often turns up a cheaper round-trip fare. Worth $200 to $400.
  • Mix camping and hotels. Camp at the parks where sites are obtainable and book hotels in the two towns where they are not. Saves $600 to $1,000 against all-hotel, and does not ask you to camp fourteen nights straight.
  • Go in the first two weeks of September. Rates in gateway towns drop hard after Labor Day while the weather holds. Saves $300 to $600 on lodging alone.
  • Cut one park. Four parks in fourteen days instead of five saves fuel, a rental day and a lodging night, and makes the trip better. It is the change we recommend most often and the one people fight hardest.

What you do not cut

Water, a proper spare tire, and a look over the vehicle before you leave the last real town. Cell service is unreliable across most of this route. Tow trucks are far off and priced accordingly. The desert does not make allowances for a plan that assumed nothing would go wrong. This line is not a budgeting decision.
